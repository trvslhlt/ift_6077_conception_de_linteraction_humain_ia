\begin{abstract}
Composers adding performance detail to a draft think in perceptual, relative terms (the strings should swell beneath the melody, the brass should hold back), yet score editors and Digital Audio Workstations (DAWs) accept only low-level inputs such as dynamic markings, velocities and automation points spread across many tracks.
We propose \textbf{ConductAir}, which reframes this work through the metaphor of a \emph{conductor directing an ensemble of autonomous musicians}.
The user conducts during playback with hand gestures and speech, and AI agents, one per instrument section, turn this direction into coordinated changes to tempo, dynamics, articulation and balance. A fast model interprets speech and gesture cues and selects how continuous motion is mapped, so gestures never wait on a model, while a large language model (LLM) plans complex instructions. Every agent move passes through a change bus that gives the user priority and records each take as editable automation, preserving direct manipulation alongside mixed-initiative delegation.
ConductAir is a test case for an approach that is not specific to music: an \emph{expressive interpretation layer} that turns expressive direction into coordinated operations on a technical interface without sacrificing precision or editability.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Musicians think about music in perceptual and expressive terms~\cite{cartwright2013socialeq}, but software accepts only technical inputs. The gap is widest when a composer adds performance detail to a first draft, because expression in an ensemble is relative: a \emph{piano} marking means something different depending on what the other instruments are playing. Realizing one idea in a DAW, such as ``let the strings swell under the melody while the brass recedes,'' requires coordinated edits to velocities and expression curves across many tracks: tedious for experts, out of reach for musicians without the technical vocabulary, and impossible in real time with two hands.

Current tools do not close this gap. Text-to-music generators such as Suno render audio from a prompt, but as a black box, and while a ``vibe'' is easy to describe in words, precise intent for a phrase is not~\cite{hammad2025vibe}. LLM assistants can execute DAW edits~\cite{elkins2025dawzy}, but one chat request at a time, apart from playback. Computer conducting systems shape a performance in real time~\cite{borchers2004personal}, but through fixed mappings from motion to a few global parameters~\cite{hunt2003mapping}.

We propose \textbf{ConductAir}, built on the \emph{performer as conductor} metaphor (Figure~\ref{fig:layers}): the user conducts their draft as it plays, and an ensemble of AI agents performs it.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Agent ensemble.} Each instrument section (e.g., strings, brass) is played by an agent that controls the dynamics, articulation and balance of its tracks; a tempo agent owns the tempo map. Relative direction (``strings over the brass'') becomes coordinated moves by several agents.
  \item \textbf{Multi-modal conductor input.} Camera hand tracking is the primary input. \emph{Continuous} motion carries expression (beat drives tempo, height drives dynamics, sharpness drives articulation); \emph{discrete} gestures carry meaning (pointing addresses a section; cues and cutoffs issue commands). As in Put-That-There~\cite{bolt1980put}, speech adds what gestures cannot say: pointing at the strings while saying ``more aggressive'' sets what hand height controls.
  \item \textbf{Interpretation at three speeds.} A gesture's audible response should arrive within about 10\,ms~\cite{wessel2001intimate}, so continuous gestures pass every frame through a gesture mapping with no model in the loop. A fast structured-output model (Jev~\cite{typesafe2026systemone}) interprets speech with recent gesture cues, choosing the sections addressed, an expressive descriptor, an intensity and the mapping to use; agents turn descriptors into parameter recipes in code (\emph{aggressive} $\rightarrow$ higher velocity, shorter notes). Compound or novel instructions go to an LLM, which returns structured recipes, mappings and coordination rules that are validated and saved for reuse.
  \item \textbf{Change bus and performance capture.} Agents propose changes to a change bus, which applies them to the project state of openDAW~\cite{michelle2026opendaw}, an open-source web DAW, and records each take as automation curves and a tempo map. When the user grabs a control in the timeline view, that parameter becomes \emph{human-held}: agent writes to it are dropped until release, and the agent adapts.
\end{itemize}

ConductAir draws on three theories seen in class. As an \emph{interface metaphor}~\cite{carroll1988metaphors}, conducting is a familiar model of directing many performers at once. Conducting gestures already carry shared meanings for tempo, dynamics, articulation and phrasing, so gestures keep the \emph{articulatory distance} small, while expressive descriptors keep the \emph{semantic distance} small~\cite{hutchins1985direct}. Delegating to agents has long been seen as in tension with \emph{direct manipulation}~\cite{shneiderman1997direct}. ConductAir keeps both: performance capture and the timeline view make every agent action visible, incremental and reversible~\cite{shneiderman1983direct}. Following \emph{mixed-initiative} principles~\cite{horvitz1999principles}, confident interpretations execute immediately, while uncertain ones await confirmation from ensuing input.

ConductAir's core is not specific to music. Video editors, lighting consoles and 3D animation tools likewise expose hundreds of technical parameters to users who think in expressive terms. ConductAir is our test case for an \emph{expressive interpretation layer}: an AI layer between a person's expressive direction and a technical interface that turns direction into coordinated low-level operations while leaving every operation visible and editable. We aim to contribute:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{An architecture for AI interpretation of real-time expressive input}, which separates per-frame control with no model in the loop from interpretation (a fast model) and planning (an LLM), so the AI decides what a gesture means without delaying it.
  \item \textbf{A mechanism for shared human--agent control of one interface}, in which every change passes through a bus that gives the human priority and lets agents adapt to overrides.
  \item \textbf{Delegation without loss of direct manipulation}: agent actions become ordinary, editable artifacts of the underlying tool, so the user can revise everything the agents did.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Our research question is whether conducting an agent ensemble reduces the effort of adding performance detail to a draft, compared with editing directly in a DAW, without reducing the user's sense of control.} We will explore it with a working prototype on a short orchestral excerpt, adding the same performance details once by conducting and once by editing in openDAW.

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Conducting and Gesture-Based Music Systems}

Computer conducting has a long history; Personal Orchestra, for example, let museum visitors conduct the tempo and dynamics of a recorded orchestra~\cite{borchers2004personal}. Such systems map motion directly to a few parameters, and those mappings stay fixed during performance~\cite{hunt2003mapping}. ConductAir keeps low-latency mapping for continuous control but adds an expressive interpretation layer above it: following multimodal interfaces since Put-That-There~\cite{bolt1980put}, a model interprets gestures with speech and decides, during performance, which sections continuous motion drives and which expressive quality it shapes.

\subsection{Semantic and AI-Assisted Music Control}

Crowdsourced descriptor maps link words such as \emph{warm} to audio effect settings~\cite{cartwright2013socialeq}, but are applied one adjustment at a time, outside performance. Loop Copilot frames an LLM as conducting an AI ensemble~\cite{zhang2023loopcopilot}, but generates new audio rather than performing an existing piece, and DAWZY turns requests into reversible DAW edits~\cite{elkins2025dawzy}, one at a time. ConductAir applies descriptor-based control in real time, driven by gesture, and keeps every change editable.

\subsection{Score and DAW Editing Tools}

Interviews with professional composers show a tension between the structure of a score and the flexibility needed to sketch and iterate, which EuterPen addresses with pen input on the score~\cite{cavez2025euterpen}. ConductAir shares this goal but changes the object of manipulation from symbols to the sounding performance, and from pen to conducting gestures. It builds on openDAW~\cite{michelle2026opendaw}, whose observable project state lets human and agent edits share one timeline.
